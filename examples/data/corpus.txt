Language models read text as sequences of tokens, and the tokenizer decides
what those tokens are. A byte pair encoding tokenizer starts from the 256
byte values and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair into a new
token. Frequent words and fragments become single tokens, while rare text
falls back to shorter pieces. The same merges must be applied in rank order
at encoding time, or the token stream drifts from the one the model saw in
training.

Compression is the practical payoff: a well trained tokenizer represents a
corpus with fewer tokens than raw bytes, which shortens sequences and saves
attention budget. But merging is a trade. A vocabulary that is too small
leaves common words split into pieces; one that is too large spends rare
merges on noise and slows every embedding lookup. Measuring bytes per token
on held out text keeps that trade honest.

Deterministic training matters for reproducibility. If two runs of the same
pipeline produce different merges, downstream token ids change silently and
cached artifacts become invalid. TokenForge counts pairs exactly, breaks
ties on the pair itself, and stores merges as plain JSON so a tokenizer can
be diffed, reviewed and rebuilt byte for byte.
